The problem

Online learning is broken at both ends.

People do not have a learning problem. They have a very specific one: they want to understand one concrete thing, properly, from someone who actually does it. Today they get pushed to one of two extremes, and neither answers the question in their head.

01 · Too passive
Recorded courses you never finish
You buy a twelve-hour course, watch forty minutes, and stop. Completion rates are famously low, and when you do get stuck, there is no one to ask. The course cannot see you.
02 · Too crowded
Webinars where you are a spectator
A free live session sounds better, until two hundred people show up. The host reads slides and answers three questions from the chat. Your hand never gets picked. It is a broadcast, not help.
03 · The real want
One thing, done in front of you
The thing people will happily pay for is small and specific: watch a practitioner do the real work for an hour, and be able to ask about your exact situation. Nothing was built for that shape.

So the brief was never build another course platform. It was: guarantee that your question gets answered.

The core bet

Make the room small on purpose.

One format, held firm. The constraint is not a limitation to work around, it is the product. A room capped at twenty is small enough that every hand gets picked, and that single decision shapes every screen that follows.

What we found, what we shipped

Five problems, five decisions in the UI.

Every doubt a person has before paying for a live session became a design problem. Here is each one we found, and the thing we actually built to answer it.

Problem 01

"Will my question really get answered?"

The whole promise rests on the room being small. But a line of copy saying "only 20 seats" is easy to skim past and easy to disbelieve. The cap was invisible.

Solution

Make the cap physical: the seat meter.

Twenty little cells, filled as people book, on every card and every booking. You can see at a glance that the room is nearly full, or that two seats stand between this session and running. The constraint became the most visible object in the UI.

Problem 02

"Is this a real working session, or a sales webinar?"

People have been burned by "live" sessions that turn out to be a pitch. Before paying, they need to know exactly what the hour contains, and that it is someone doing the work, not talking about it.

Solution

A minute-by-minute agenda on every session.

Each session page breaks the hour into blocks, 0 to 10, 10 to 30, 30 to 40, 40 to 60, plus plain answers to who it is for, what you need, and which device to use. The expectation is set in full before any money moves.

Problem 03

"Booking a live thing feels risky."

Three separate fears, all real: I will lose my seat while the UPI app loads. What if the session is not good. And the worst, what if it does not even run and I am left chasing a refund.

Solution

Remove the risk at every step, visibly.

The seat is held for ten minutes during payment. Full refund up to 24 hours before. And if fewer than five book, the session moves a week and auto-refunds, surfaced as a calm "needs your decision" card, never an email you miss.

Problem 04

"What if I teach to an empty room?"

A marketplace dies if the supply side is scared. A practitioner will not block an evening and prepare for an hour if three people might show up and the payout is a mystery.

Solution

De-risk the teacher with the same floor, plus clarity.

The five-to-run floor protects teachers too: below it, Skedo moves the date automatically. A dashboard shows held earnings released two days after each session, live attendance, a green room to start from, and a poster to help fill the last seats.

Problem 05

"Is the certificate worth anything?"

Attendance certificates are usually theatre, handed out for buying a ticket. That makes them worthless to show and risky to trust, and it exposes teachers to recordings being leaked.

Solution

Earn it by showing up, prove it with a link.

A certificate is issued only if you are present for at least 42 of 60 minutes, measured by the platform. It carries a public verification link, and we say plainly it records attendance, not accreditation. Recordings are watermarked with the viewer's email.

The design language

Warm and calm, so a dense marketplace feels trustworthy.

Ticketing screens are usually loud. Skedo is the opposite: a warm paper base, a single deep green for action, an editorial serif, and status written as plain sentences rather than a wall of badges. The job of the UI is to make money, time zones, seats and refunds feel calm.

Where it lands

A format that only works if trust is built in.

Skedo is a live concept in beta. The design is not judged on a launch number but on whether both sides feel safe enough to show up. These are the things the product is built to move, and what I would watch.

Fill rate to the floor
What share of sessions reach five seats and run. The whole move-or-refund system exists to protect this moment.
Attendance, not just sales
Because the certificate is gated on minutes present, attendance becomes a first-class metric, for learners and for teacher credibility.
Refund and no-show rate
Clear agendas, device guidance and the ten-minute hold are all aimed at the quiet killers of a live marketplace.
Repeat and teacher retention
The real test: do learners book a second hour, and do teachers come back once they have been paid cleanly and filled a room.
